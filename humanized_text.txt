Satisfaction as the Conduit: How User Engagement and Experience Create Brand Value in the Metaverse

Abstract
Brands keep planting flags in immersive digital worlds, yet what actually sustains their performance in the metaverse stays an under-theorised question. This study draws on Flow Theory and Self-Determination Theory (SDT) to build and empirically test a configurational model that links user engagement (UE) and user experience (UX) to brand satisfaction (BSAT) and brand success (BSUC) across metaverse ecosystems. We use a mixed-method design: partial least squares structural equation modelling (PLS-SEM) validates the measurement, and fuzzy-set qualitative comparative analysis (fsQCA) handles the causal complexity. The data come from 312 active metaverse users in India, a strategically important yet under-represented emerging market. Engagement alone isn't sufficient for brand success. Experience alone isn't either. What matters are specific configurations particularly brand satisfaction sitting alongside either experience or engagement and these are associated with favourable outcomes. The fully triadic configuration (BSAT x UX x UE) yields the highest solution consistency, evidencing equifinality in metaverse branding. On theory, the study extends Flow Theory and SDT into co-creative, technology-mediated brand ecosystems. For managers, its configurational logic gives strategists practical guidance for designing identity-affirming, community-embedded virtual experiences that sustain engagement and competitive differentiation.

Keywords:  Metaverse; User engagement ;User experience ;Brand satisfaction ;Brand success; fsQCA

Introduction:
The metaverse is an immersive digital space built on augmented and virtual reality (AR/VR), where people interact with one another and with virtual objects while digital information blends into the physical world (Arya et al., 2024a; Zallio and Clarkson, 2022). It's reshaping how brands engage consumers. Traditional marketing gives way to digital experiences that deliver measurable value (Arya et al., 2024a; Hennig-Thurau et al., 2023; Anderson et al., 2024).
Conventional digital channels can't do what the metaverse does. It creates multisensory brand experiences that blur the line between physical and digital, tying individual engagement to wider social systems and demanding adaptive strategies from any brand that wants to stay competitive (Kopalle et al., 2024; Marvi et al., 2024; Bilgihan et al., 2024; Hwang and Seo, 2025). Academic guidance on folding it into business strategy is still thin (Eggenschwiler et al., 2024). Take Nike's Nikeland on Roblox: interactive mini-games and rewards pull users in, and it shows how virtual environments can redefine marketing (Kumar, 2024; Kozinets et al., 2023).
Branding here differs from website or app branding in four ways, and those differences are what push us toward a configurational approach. Environments are persistent, shared three-dimensional spaces. Users act through avatars, so identity and embodiment fuse with consumption. Interaction is socially synchronous, which creates co-presence with other people and with brands in real time. And value is frequently co-created. Brand outcomes emerge from combinations of these traits, not from any single attribute, so we adopt a configurational lens and ask which recipes of engagement, experience and satisfaction jointly produce brand success.
Emotional connection matters here. Emotional attachment correlates with stronger brand relationships (Bilgihan et al., 2024; Arya et al., 2022), and immersive, personalised interactions build satisfaction, trust and brand identification (Shamim et al., 2025; Vo et al., 2025).
Brands can also break past physical limits and reach global audiences, and personalisation strengthens both perceptions and satisfaction (Dwivedi et al., 2023; Chandra et al., 2022). From there, the study asks a central question: how do immersive, avatar-mediated interactions shape the configurations of user engagement, user experience and brand satisfaction that lead to brand success? Two sub-questions follow. Which combinations are sufficient for high brand success? And what role does brand satisfaction play in linking engagement and experience to success?
We make three contributions. First, instead of re-confirming that engagement, experience and satisfaction matter, the study specifies how immersive, avatar-based environments change their configuration, arguing that equifinality the existence of multiple sufficient pathways to success runs especially deep in the metaverse. Second, it brings Flow Theory and SDT together as complementary lenses that explain why immersive interactions sustain engagement and shape satisfaction. Third, through fsQCA, it pins down the specific combinations of engagement, experience and satisfaction associated with high brand success, and hands managers configurational guidance.
The study extends Flow Theory and SDT to immersive digital ecosystems (Fan et al., 2022).
Our account is configurational: user engagement (UE) and user experience (UX) are antecedents of brand satisfaction (BSAT), which is in turn associated with brand success (BSUC). We examine how these combine rather than treating them as isolated drivers.

2. Literature Review:
2.1. Immersive Environment
Firms can improve metaverse experiences through three components: immersiveness, sociability and ecological faithfulness (Arya et al., 2024a). Immersiveness captures mental absorption and sensory engagement, often called "presence". It strengthens emotional engagement and connection, and through that, user satisfaction and brand trust (Shamim et al., 2025; Daassi et al., 2021; Bilgihan et al., 2024).
Sociability the quality of social interaction opens the door to collaboration, community building and emotional bonding, and social presence lifts enjoyment and advocacy (Hennig-Thurau et al., 2023; Osborne et al., 2024). Ecological faithfulness, the authenticity of virtual environments, builds trust among environmentally conscious consumers (Dwivedi et al., 2023).
Weaving together immersive storytelling, gamification and virtual product launches is a real growth avenue (Bilgihan et al., 2024; Blazquez, 2024; Lee and Shen, 2024). Such experiences can build loyalty (Rather et al., 2022), but a poorly executed design may trigger frustration, overstimulation or escapism, which is why psychologically safe, user-centred strategies matter (Lavoie et al., 2021; Duong et al., 2025).

2.2 Conceptualization of User Engagement in The Metaverse
No single definition has settled the matter, yet most accounts describe the metaverse as a three-dimensional virtual shared world powered by AR/VR, one that lets people move smoothly between physical and virtual domains for work, interaction and socialisation (Buhalis et al., 2023; Koohang et al., 2023; Kozinets et al., 2023). Figure 1 shows how physical and virtual environments feed into each other and underpin brand success.
Building on early engagement theory, O'Brien and Toms (2010) developed a validated, multifaceted engagement scale, while the Mobile User Engagement model explains engagement through motivation, perceived value and satisfaction (Soltani et al., 2024; Ilkan et al., 2023).
Others pushed in different directions: holistic Quality of Experience conceptualisations (Lycett and Radwan, 2019), multi-instrumental approaches (Kyriakou et al., 2024), and extensions into e-government contexts (Li and Shang, 2023; Zhang et al., 2022). Shin (2019) tied AR-game immersion to cognitive and motivational constructs.

2.3 Impact of User Behaviours on Brand Engagement in Virtual Environments
What users actually do buying NFTs, interacting socially, attending virtual events, creating content sits at the centre of brand engagement in immersive ecosystems. These behaviours shape perceptions, build emotional bonds and drive loyalty (Shamim et al., 2025; Bilgihan et al., 2024; Lee and Shen, 2024).
Branded NFTs open up digital self-expression, exclusivity and identity construction, and owning unique avatars, wearables and collectibles strengthens emotional attachment (Xie and Muralidharan, 2023; Simonetti et al., 2025). Scarcity and perceived investment value lift brand attitudes further, and social-comparison dynamics amplify the effect (Wang et al., 2024; Chang et al., 2024; Xie et al., 2024).
Social interaction reinforces engagement. Brand communities build trust, knowledge-sharing and purchase intention, and avatar-based interactivity deepens belonging and loyalty (Agnihotri et al., 2024; Kumar and Shankar, 2024; Payal et al., 2024).
Virtual events and gamified rewards build trust, satisfaction and loyalty, especially among Gen Z (Habachi et al., 2024), and participatory content creation deepens ownership and advocacy (Bilgihan et al., 2024; Xu et al., 2024).

2.4 Impact of user engagement and experience on brand success in virtual environments
User engagement and experience are major drivers of brand success. Wiertz and De Ruyter (2007) showed that contributions to firm-hosted online communities lift brand success, with knowledge-sharing doing much of the engagement work.
Brands have to actively make interaction happen if they want engagement to rise (Huang and Benyoucef, 2013). Culture matters too: holistic thinkers judge brand extensions more favourably in virtual settings (Su et al., 2021).
Social media is woven into engagement, and stronger social identification and interactivity raise engagement with branded content (Hsieh and Tsao, 2019).
On the theory side, Brodie et al. (2011) cast engagement as active participation in brand experiences, and purchase intentions in virtual communities often serve social and self-identity needs, which frames engagement as a route to loyalty.

Theoretical Foundation
3.1 Flow theory
Flow Theory (Csikszentmihalyi, 1990) gives us a strong framework for reading metaverse engagement. Flow is a state of deep immersion and focused attention, marked by high intrinsic motivation and enjoyment, and it's especially relevant where engagement decides brand success. Recent work carries it into virtual and augmented reality: in game-based metaverses, perceived enjoyment strengthens flow, which feeds satisfaction and continued use (Hyeon and Lee, 2025).
Current research keeps tying flow to virtual brand experiences. Authenticity, interactivity and entertainment strengthen flow in live streaming, and interactivity carries the most weight (Wu et al., 2024). Technological factors drive flow in emerging-market metaverse use (Liang et al., 2024). Flow speeds up subjective time in VR (Rutrecht et al., 2021), and it shapes participative and sharing behaviours (Lo and Lai, 2023). Our survey built in flow elements clear goals, immediate feedback and challenge-skill balance and asked users to self-report flow during metaverse interactions.

Self-Determination Theory (SDT)
Self-Determination Theory (SDT) holds that motivation and well-being peak when three innate needs are met: autonomy (volitional choice), competence (feeling effective) and relatedness (feeling connected) (Deci and Ryan, 2000). In digital environments, SDT explains how the intrinsic drive to explore, master and connect pushes engagement deeper.
The two theories fit together. Flow captures moment-to-moment immersion when challenge-skill balance, clear goals and feedback line up (Csikszentmihalyi, 1990), and SDT explains why users enter that state and stay in it: autonomy supports exploration, competence drives mastery, and relatedness keeps social interaction going through avatars. Between them, they account for the immersion and need satisfaction that sit under engagement, satisfaction and loyalty.
3.3 Constructs and Hypotheses
Four constructs are in play. User engagement (UE) is the cognitive, emotional and behavioural involvement a user has with a brand during metaverse interactions. User experience (UX) is the perceived quality of that interaction immersion, usability, enjoyment and presence. Brand satisfaction (BSAT) is the user's overall evaluation of the brand from metaverse encounters. Brand success (BSUC) covers brand-relevant outcomes: continuance intention, purchase intention, advocacy and word of mouth. We treat the SDT needs and Flow dimensions as underlying psychological mechanisms, not as separately measured constructs.
Read together, Flow Theory and SDT imply that immersive, need-satisfying interactions raise engagement and experience, which then shape satisfaction and brand outcomes. We therefore expect higher user engagement to be positively associated with brand success (H1), and higher user experience quality to be positively associated with brand success (H2). Satisfaction sums up cumulative evaluations, so we expect brand satisfaction to mediate the engagement/experience-success relationships (H3). And in keeping with a configurational view, high brand success may arise from multiple sufficient configurations rather than any single necessary condition (P1).

4. Methodology
4.1 Fuzzy set Qualitative Comparative Analysis and its Application to the study
Fuzzy-set Qualitative Comparative Analysis (fsQCA) gives us a mixed-method framework for studying complex causal configurations in branding (Jia et al., 2022). It sits between exploratory and hypothesis-testing strategies, showing how combinations of conditions present or absent jointly produce an outcome, and it surfaces equifinality (Schneider and Wagemann, 2012). It earns its keep in small-N research (Ragin, 2008).
The process runs through six stages: case selection, identification of causal conditions, calibration of membership scores, truth-table construction, logical minimisation, and robustness checks through consistency and coverage. Figure 2 lays out this flow. Boolean minimisation derives the minimal sufficient combinations, exposing causal complexity that single-variable effects would miss.

4.2 Identification and selection of variables
Variable selection followed a structured three-stage process. A literature review came first, pulling out consistently validated variables satisfaction, interaction frequency, perceived brand impact, emotional connection and personalisation as core indicators (Cadet and Chainay, 2020; Lu and Lau, 2025).
Second, interviews and focus groups gave a finer read on involvement, satisfaction drivers and brand-success perceptions, and the variables were refined accordingly. Third, surveys captured UE, UX, BSAT and BSUC (Table 2); immersiveness was measured via presence scales, sociability via social and co-presence measures, and ecological faithfulness via authenticity measures (Cadet and Chainay, 2020; Poeschl and Doering, 2015; Lu and Lau, 2025).
Only constructs with strong conceptual grounding and proven reliability were retained, and a pilot study validated clarity and reliability before full-scale administration.

4.3 Measures
All constructs were measured on seven-point Likert scales anchored from 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree). The ten-item engagement and experience scales are adapted subsets. Appendix Table 1 lists the full measurement items alongside their adapted sources.

4.4 Platforms Studied and Metaverse Activities
We focused on three prominent platforms Roblox, Decentraland and The Sandbox chosen for their adoption, functionality and capacity to host brand interactions. Roblox, known for user-generated content and social gaming, hosts branded experiences such as Nike's Nikeland (Bilgihan et al., 2024). Decentraland, built on blockchain, lets users buy virtual land, trade NFTs and attend community events (Brouard, 2024).
The Sandbox supports voxel-based creation and monetisation, and brands such as Adidas use it (Brandes and Dolp, 2025). Across the platforms, four activity types were considered: trading digital assets, attending virtual events, avatar-based social interaction, and designing digital assets or experiences (Lee and Shen, 2024; Hennig-Thurau et al., 2023).

4.5 Data Collection
Data were collected using stratified random sampling. From a frame of adults (18+) active on at least one platform with prior brand-related metaverse experience, 600 eligible users were invited at random, stratified by primary activity (gaming, NFT trading, social interaction, content creation and virtual events). Of these, 312 provided complete, consenting responses a 52% response rate with balanced representation, measured on validated UE, UX, BSAT and BSUC scales.
India is a theoretically and empirically justified context. It's one of the world's fastest-growing metaverse markets (USD 2 billion in 2023, projected to grow at a robust CAGR through 2030), with a large, digitally native youth population (median age 28), rapid 5G expansion and high mobile-first penetration (Verma, 2024; Statista, 2025; Dwivedi et al., 2023). Indian consumers take readily to avatar-based self-expression and NFT ownership, which makes the setting especially relevant, and studying this underexplored, non-Western market widens the theoretical reach and generalisability of the findings (Payal et al., 2024).

5.2. Assessment of Reliability and validity
PLS-SEM confirmed satisfactory reliability and consistency. The measurement model was evaluated via standardized loadings, Cronbach's alpha, composite reliability (CR) and average variance extracted (AVE). Standardized loadings ranged 0.699-0.741 (Brand Satisfaction), 0.670-0.707 (User Engagement), 0.677-0.714 (User Experience) and 0.661-0.682 (Brand Success), all exceeding the 0.60 minimum with significant z-values (p < .001) (Bagozzi and Yi, 1988; Table 2).
Cronbach's alpha and CR came in at 0.84 (BSAT), 0.90 (UE), 0.90 (UX) and 0.77 (BSUC), all above the 0.70 minimum (Nunnally and Bernstein, 1994; Hair et al., 2019), which indicates that the items measure their constructs consistently.
For convergent validity, every loading was positive and significant (p < .001), and CR ranged 0.77-0.90 (Table 2). AVE values were 0.51 (BSAT), 0.48 (UE), 0.48 (UX) and 0.45 (BSUC). Three sit marginally below 0.50, but convergent validity still holds given the significant loadings and adequate CR (Fornell and Larcker, 1981).
Common method bias was assessed with Harman's single-factor test (Podsakoff et al., 2003): one unrotated factor explained 31.4% of variance, below 50%. Procedural remedies anonymity, no right/wrong answers, and separation of predictor and criterion items further limited bias, though it cannot be entirely ruled out in cross-sectional self-report data.
Discriminant validity used the Heterotrait-Monotrait (HTMT) ratio (Hair et al., 2019; Henseler et al., 2015). All HTMT values fell below the 0.85 threshold (maximum 0.814 for the engagement-experience pair; Table 2a), confirming each construct is empirically distinct. Read alongside the Harman result, neither method bias nor poor discriminant validity threatens the conclusions.

5.3. Data Analysis using FsQCA
5.3.1. Data Driven Results from FsQCA
To analyse UE, UX, BSAT and BSUC, raw responses were calibrated (Ragin, 2008) into fuzzy-set membership scores, each expressing how far a case belongs to a condition and to the outcome.
Calibration used three anchors: the 95th percentile for full membership (0.95), the 50th as the crossover point (0.50), and the 5th for full non-membership (0.05), which keeps scores between 0 and 1 (Ragin, 2008; Douglas et al., 2020). This shared scale lets us compare the calibrated UE, UX, BSAT and BSUC values (Table 3).

Tables 5 and 6 make the equifinality plain: several distinct paths reach the same outcome (Ragin, 2008). No single factor BSAT, UX or UE is necessary on its own; it's their interaction that drives brand outcomes in immersive digital environments.
Table 5 gives three core configurations for high BSUC. Configuration 1 (BSAT + UX) shows brand success even without strong engagement, since satisfied users with positive immersive experiences form lasting relationships (Bilgihan et al., 2024). Configuration 2 (BSAT + UE) pairs satisfaction with active participation, lifting loyalty and advocacy (Hollebeek et al., 2022). Configuration 3 (UX + UE) shows that even without reported satisfaction, immersive interactive environments yield favourable outcomes, which supports Flow Theory (Csikszentmihalyi, 1990).
Table 6 widens the picture through subset-superset analysis. Configuration A, the "Triple Path" (BSAT, UX, UE), records the highest combined coverage (0.305): satisfied, engaged and immersed users push brand success to its peak, in step with service-dominant logic and value co-creation.
The other configurations show room to manoeuvre. B (BSAT + UX) and D (BSAT + UE) reaffirm satisfaction combined with either experience or engagement, so brands can tailor their strategies. C (UX + UE), despite lower consistency, backs immersive participatory experiences even without explicit satisfaction (Shin, 2019).
5.3.2. Necessity Analysis
The necessity analysis tests whether any single condition has to be present; a condition counts as necessary only when its consistency tops 0.90 (Schneider and Wagemann, 2012). None cleared that bar: UE, UX and BSAT recorded single-condition consistency of 0.373, 0.362 and 0.410 respectively. High brand success comes from combinations, then, not from any single necessary condition which is what sends us to the sufficiency analysis.

5.3.3. Sufficiency Analysis
The sufficiency analysis identifies which configurations are jointly sufficient. UE, UX and BSAT were entered into the truth table with consistency and frequency thresholds applied (Ragin, 2008; Schneider and Wagemann, 2012). As Tables 5 and 6 report, brand satisfaction turns up in every high-consistency sufficient path, while engagement and experience contribute in combination rather than in isolation.
The three sufficient configurations sit well with prior evidence. BSAT x UX records high coverage and consistency: satisfied users who enjoy well-designed experiences stay loyal and recommend the brand. BSAT x UE looks similarly favourable, consistent with engagement-loyalty links (Jeong and Kim, 2019) and social-media effects on customer equity (Ebrahim, 2020). UX x UE contributes at slightly lower consistency, which reflects how experience and engagement work together at the heart of brand success in immersive settings.
User engagement here is multifaceted asset trading, event attendance, social interaction, content creation and it reflects psychological, social and experiential motivations, which the UE and UX constructs capture rather than any platform-specific variables.

5.4 Structural Model and Hypothesis Evaluation
fsQCA tests sufficiency, not net linear effects, so the hypotheses are evaluated configurationally (Table 7). In line with the necessity result, user engagement (single-condition consistency 0.373) and user experience (0.362) aren't sufficient alone; they support brand success only in combination, which backs H1 and H2 within sufficient configurations. Brand satisfaction shows up in every high-consistency path the triadic BSAT x UX x UE (0.713), BSAT x UE (0.669) and BSAT x UX (0.650) and posts the highest single-condition consistency (0.410), a sign of the central, mediation-consistent role that supports H3. A formal bootstrap mediation test would make a useful complement.

6. Discussion
The fsQCA findings clarify how combinations of UE, UX and BSAT contribute to brand success in the metaverse, and they mark out the main pathways to favourable outcomes.
BSAT x UX came through as a significant configuration (consistency 0.650, raw coverage 20.84%): satisfied users who perceive high-quality immersive experiences build loyalty and advocacy, consistent with prior work on satisfaction, experience and emotional connection (Thaichon et al., 2022; Bilgihan et al., 2024). Brands should prioritise seamless immersive experiences while facing up to ethical concerns such as data-privacy risks and excessive use.
BSAT x UE showed a strong association (consistency 0.669, coverage 24.15%): active participation with high satisfaction improves outcomes, echoing engagement-equity links (Jeong and Kim, 2019). Brands should encourage gamified and co-creation activities that deepen involvement while keeping satisfaction high.
UX x UE, though slightly lower in consistency (0.600, coverage 21.90%), shows that engagement works best when it's paired with high-quality experience even where satisfaction isn't the dominant condition, consistent with Flow Theory's challenge-skill balance (Csikszentmihalyi, 1990; Shin, 2019).
Individual factors scored lower consistency than their combinations, which argues for holistic strategies: BSAT x UX x UE reached the highest consistency (0.713), reinforcing Flow Theory's premise that optimal engagement grows out of a balance between challenge and skill in immersive environments (Csikszentmihalyi, 1990).

7. Implications
7.1 Theoretical Contributions
This research brings SDT and Flow Theory together inside immersive, digitally mediated environments defined by persistent communities, avatar-based identity and interactive interfaces, and it clarifies how psychological needs and experiential states jointly shape engagement, satisfaction and brand outcomes. Earlier applications sat in traditional or gamified settings; metaverse interactions instead blend intrinsic motivation and experiential absorption in a distinctive way, which carries both theories into co-created domains.
Within SDT, autonomy, competence and relatedness take on distinctive forms here: autonomy through customisable avatars and digital-asset ownership, competence through skill-based challenges and achievement, and relatedness through persistent social interaction in branded communities (Bilgihan et al., 2024). We advance these as conceptual mechanisms behind engagement and experience, not as separately measured effects.
We extend Flow Theory by proposing that experiential immersion depends on matching task challenge to user skill, tuned by digital affordances and interaction design (Zhong and Hamouda, 2024); gamified missions and event-based interactions are the flow facilitators. In keeping with our findings, deep immersive engagement can yield favourable outcomes even when satisfaction isn't the dominant condition.
Taken together, the study moves marketing and psychological theory forward by suggesting that the confluence of need satisfaction and experiential immersion is associated with engagement, satisfaction and brand performance. It extends SDT and Flow Theory into co-created digital domains, and it keeps tested associations separate from theoretical interpretation.

7.2. Managerial Implications
User experience is a major determinant of both satisfaction and favourable outcomes, and it shows up again and again in the configurations associated with success. Crafting engaging, interactive and personalised metaverse experiences is therefore central to sustaining engagement and long-term brand success.
Brands should build immersive experiences tied to users' virtual identities. Personalisation customisable avatars, interactive environments lets users express who they are and strengthens presence, which reinforces the emotional bond. When a brand's virtual presence matches users' self-concept, connection grows; tailored experiences deepen engagement, loyalty and advocacy (Thaichon et al., 2022; Xie and Muralidharan, 2023).
Features that come up often NFT interoperability, smart contracts, rarity dynamics may strengthen exclusivity and trust, but we didn't measure them here, so we present them as future avenues rather than findings. The evidence speaks to combinations of engagement, experience and satisfaction, not to blockchain-specific mechanisms.
The results show synergy between satisfaction and engagement: pairing participation with high satisfaction strengthens equity and loyalty (Jeong and Kim, 2019). Managers should build gamified, co-creation and reward opportunities that deepen involvement, because a user's sense of ownership reinforces attachment and long-term loyalty.
User experience sits at the centre of both satisfaction and success. To stay competitive, brands have to make immersive experiences emotionally resonant, consistent and matched to users' values and identities (Jung and Pawlowski, 2014); functional or visually appealing assets no longer cut it. Brands that deliver high-quality interactive experiences build lasting engagement, loyalty and competitive advantage.

8. Limitations and Future Scope
The study carries several limitations. The sample of 312 active, experienced users guarantees expertise, but it may tilt results toward highly engaged participants and underrepresents casual or novice users, which limits generalisability; a more diverse, representative sampling frame is needed.
Inclusion and exclusion decisions may have missed relevant factors, and the lack of a control group limits causal attribution. fsQCA handles complex causal analysis well, yet its small-N, qualitative orientation trims robustness next to larger quantitative models and limits methodological triangulation.
The cross-sectional design can't track how engagement and experience change over time; longitudinal studies are needed to test whether engagement holds up. Ethical questions privacy, addiction, cyberbullying, exploitation stay underexplored, and future work should build safeguards for user well-being.
Several large technology firms have pulled back their metaverse investments, so we frame our contribution conservatively. The findings speak most directly to brands active on large, metaverse-like platforms game-based worlds such as Roblox and Fortnite, and social virtual reality and to the design of future immersive environments. We treat avatar-mediated branding as an evolving phenomenon whose psychological mechanisms engagement, experience and satisfaction are likely to persist across successive technological forms.
